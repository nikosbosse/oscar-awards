# Storytelling Improvements

## Central question

The site should explore: **"How predictable are the Oscars, and what can precursor awards tell us?"**

This is an analytical investigation into predictability itself, not a betting guide or confident forecast.

## Hook

Lead with the most surprising analytical finding, not a prediction. Examples:

- "The Directors Guild has predicted the Best Director Oscar 85% of the time over 26 years. Other categories aren't even close."
- "Some Oscar races are essentially decided weeks before the ceremony. Others are genuinely unpredictable."

## Section order

Reorder to follow a natural analytical arc — evidence first, conclusions last:

1. **Hook** — A surprising finding that pulls readers in
2. **Context** — What are precursor awards? (shortened version of current intro)
3. **The big picture** — Accuracy heatmap ("Some precursors are eerily accurate, others are noise")
4. **Go deeper** — Precursor counts, combinations, agreement matrix ("The signal gets stronger in combinations")
5. **This year's season** — 2026 at a glance ("Here's what's happening right now")
6. **What the data suggests** — Base rate model, explicitly framed as "if we naively trust historical patterns, here's what falls out"

The predictions section becomes the natural conclusion of the analysis rather than a bold claim up front.

## Lean into uncertainty

Split categories into predictability tiers:

- **Nearly decided** — categories where precursors historically predict with 80%+ accuracy
- **Competitive** — 50-80%, genuinely uncertain
- **Anyone's guess** — categories where precursors are weak signals

This gives structure to the predictions without overselling confidence.

## Frame the model with humility

Make the framing prominent: "This isn't a forecast — it's a question: if historical patterns hold, what do they suggest? Here's what a naive model produces."

## Add connective tissue

Sections currently stand alone. Add 1-2 bridging sentences between them:

- After accuracy heatmap: "But accuracy alone doesn't tell the full story. What happens when precursors disagree? And does winning more of them actually matter?"
- Before combinations: "Individual precursor accuracy is useful, but the real signal comes from specific combinations. Some are nearly bulletproof..."
- Before the forecast: "Now let's apply everything we've learned to this year's races."

## Highlight surprising findings

Add callout boxes for notable insights:

- "The DGA has been wrong only 4 times in 26 years for Best Director"
- "Nominees who sweep all major guild awards have won the Oscar 100% of the time"
- "Critics circles are terrible at predicting Best Picture — but occasionally spot winners everyone else misses"
- "Best Animated Feature is the hardest to predict from precursors"

## More evocative section titles

Current titles are descriptive but clinical. Alternatives:

| Current | Alternative |
|---|---|
| Precursor Accuracy by Oscar Category | Which Precursors Actually Matter? |
| Precursor Count vs Oscar Win Probability | Does Sweeping the Precursors Guarantee an Oscar? |
| Winning Precursor Combinations | The Combinations That (Almost) Never Lose |
| How Often Do Precursor Awards Agree? | When the Awards Agree — and When They Don't |
| 2026 Oscar Predictions — A Simple Base Rate Model | What the Data Suggests for 2026 |

## Add a historical upsets section

A short section on years when precursors got it wrong (e.g., Crash over Brokeback Mountain, Green Book over Roma). This:

- Builds credibility by not overselling the model
- Creates tension in the analysis
- Is analytically honest about the limits of the approach
