Which Award Shows Best Predict the Oscars?

Not all precursor awards are created equal. Industry guild awards — where voters overlap significantly with Oscar voters — tend to be far more predictive than critics' organizations.

Each bar shows how often a precursor's winner matches the eventual Oscar winner, averaged across all categories where that precursor competes. The accuracy percentages come from 26 years of historical matchups (2000–2025).

Industry guilds (DGA, PGA, SAG) consistently rank highest because their voting memberships overlap substantially with Academy members—the same people who vote for guild awards also vote for Oscars. Festival awards (Cannes, Venice, etc.) score near 0% because they happen months before Oscar contenders are even released, and they reward a very different type of filmmaking.
  • Longer bar = higher accuracy. A 60% bar means that precursor's winner matched the Oscar winner 60% of the time.
  • Hover over any bar to see the exact accuracy figure and number of matchups.

How Has Predictability Changed Over Time?

Oscar races have gotten more unpredictable in some categories, while others remain steady. Explore the rolling accuracy of each precursor over the past two decades.

Each line tracks a precursor's prediction accuracy using a 5-year rolling window, smoothing out year-to-year noise so you can spot meaningful trends. A rising line means that precursor is becoming more predictive; a falling line means it's losing its edge.

When Do Award Shows Agree?

Some award shows march in lockstep; others go their own way. This matrix reveals the hidden alliances between precursor awards.

This is a pairwise agreement matrix: each cell shows how often two precursor awards picked the same winner for the selected Oscar category. It is not about Oscar accuracy—it's about how often two shows agree with each other.

High agreement between industry awards (PGA, DGA, SAG) reflects their shared voter pools—many of the same professionals vote for all three. Low agreement between industry guilds and critics' circles (NYFCC, LAFCA, NBR) reveals fundamentally different aesthetic values: guild voters reward crowd-pleasing craft, while critics often champion more adventurous or international work.
  • Darker cells = more frequent agreement between two precursors on who should win.
  • The diagonal is always 100% (every show agrees with itself).
  • Use the category dropdown to see how alliances shift across different Oscar races.

The Upset Gallery

Sometimes the Oscar goes to a nominee that nobody saw coming. These are the biggest surprises — ranked by how many precursors got it wrong.

An "upset" means the Oscar winner was not the consensus frontrunner from precursor awards. The surprise meter on each card shows what percentage of precursors picked someone else—higher means more shocking. A 90% surprise means 9 out of 10 precursors predicted a different winner.

Some of the biggest upsets in Oscar history include Crash beating Brokeback Mountain for Best Picture (2006), and Shakespeare in Love toppling Saving Private Ryan (1999). These aren't just trivia—they reveal the limits of precursor data. Factors like campaign spending, late-breaking controversies, and preferential ballot mechanics can all override the precursor signal.

Which Categories Are Most Predictable?

Best Director is nearly a sure bet if you know the DGA winner. Best Picture? Not so much. Here's how each Oscar category stacks up.

For each Oscar category, two data points are shown: the best single precursor's accuracy (the gold dot) and the average accuracy across all precursors (the darker dot). The gap between them tells an important story.

  • Gold dot = the single most accurate precursor for that category (the "best predictor").
  • Dark dot = the average accuracy across all precursors that cover that category.
  • When the two dots are far apart, one dominant predictor stands out from the pack (e.g., DGA for Best Director).
  • When both dots are low, the category is genuinely unpredictable—no precursor has cracked it.
Categories where the gold and dark dots cluster together near the top (like Best Director) are the most "solved"—most precursors do well. Categories where both dots are low (like Best Original Screenplay or certain technical categories) are the wild cards where upsets are common and prediction models struggle most.

Does Consensus Equal Certainty?

When most precursors agree on a winner, how often does the Oscar follow? The relationship is surprisingly strong — but not absolute.

The X-axis measures precursor consensus—what percentage of precursors agree on the same frontrunner. The Y-axis shows how often that frontrunner actually won the Oscar. Each point represents a specific category-year combination.

  • X-axis (Consensus): the share of precursors that picked the same nominee as their winner. 100% = every precursor agreed.
  • Y-axis (Oscar Win Rate): how often the consensus pick actually won the Oscar at that level of agreement.
  • Points in the upper-right are the "locks"—high consensus, high win rate. Points in the lower-left are the chaotic races.
The critical threshold is around 70%: when more than 70% of precursors agree on a frontrunner, that nominee wins the Oscar the vast majority of the time. Below 50% consensus, it's essentially a coin flip—these are the races where upsets happen and pundits get humbled.

Battle of the Prediction Models

We tested eight different prediction models — from simple majority votes to gradient boosting — using leave-one-year-out cross-validation. Here's how they compare.

Each model was evaluated using leave-one-year-out backtesting: to predict year N, the model trains on all other years and predicts year N blind. This simulates real-world forecasting and prevents overfitting. The bar chart ranks models by overall accuracy; the table below provides detailed metrics.

  • Accuracy = percentage of categories where the model's top pick matched the actual Oscar winner.
  • Brier Score = measures prediction quality (lower is better). It penalizes confident wrong predictions more harshly. Range: 0 (perfect) to 1 (worst).
  • ECE (Expected Calibration Error) = measures whether a model's stated confidence matches reality. If a model says "80% confident" and wins 80% of the time, ECE is low (well-calibrated).
The calibration chart below is crucial for trusting a model's predictions. Points that fall on the diagonal line mean the model is well-calibrated: when it says 70%, the outcome happens about 70% of the time. Points above the line mean the model is underconfident (wins more often than it predicts). Points below mean it's overconfident (wins less often than it claims).
